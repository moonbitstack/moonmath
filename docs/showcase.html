§ 1

What the page calls

Everything on this page comes from one call. The page passes five numbers — the field of view in degrees, the eye's distance along z, the two planes and the aspect ratio of the sheet — and the library answers with the matrix and the projected corners.

the whole of it 
let eye = @moonmath.Vec3::new(1.6, 1.4, distance)
let view = @moonmath.Mat4::look_at(eye, @moonmath.Vec3::zero(), @moonmath.Vec3::unit_y())
let projection = @moonmath.Mat4::perspective(
  @moonmath.to_radians(field_of_view),
  aspect,
  near,
  far,
)
let view_projection = projection * view

// Eight corners of a cube from -1 to 1, projected one at a time.
let ndc = view_projection.transform_point(corner)

The bridge that carries the answer across is a dozen lines in src/examples/web/: it writes the sixteen numbers and the eight points into a string the page can read. Nothing is reimplemented on the other side — the ink is the only thing the page adds.

watched, not assumed

Move the far dial and the cube does not move. The first two rows of the projection do not mention \(f\): it takes part only in depth and in clipping. The curve, which is all depth, moves a great deal.

The dashed edges are the three that meet at the corner facing away from the eye: the page tests each face's normal against the eye, and leaves what it cannot see to a dashed line, as a draughtsman would.

§ 2

Why the curve bends

Depth is a projection of a projection. The clip-space \(z\) is affine in the distance, and then it is divided by \(w\), which is the distance itself — so what arrives is a hyperbola:

$$ z_{\text{ndc}}(d) = \frac{f}{f-n} - \frac{fn}{f-n}\cdot\frac{1}{d} $$

At the near plane \(d = n\) the two terms cancel and the depth is zero; at the far plane \(d = f\) the second term is \(n/(f-n)\) and what is left is one. Between them, the curve is already at nine tenths of its height when the distance is ten times the near plane, whatever the far plane is:

$$ \frac{f}{f-n} - \frac{fn}{f-n}\cdot\frac{1}{10n} = \frac{f}{f-n}\left(1 - \frac{1}{10}\right) = 0.9\cdot\frac{f}{f-n} $$

This is the reason a depth buffer spends its precision where the eye can tell the difference, and the reason a near plane of 0.01 costs more than it looks: it buys a hundredth of a metre and charges the whole buffer for it.

Set near to 0.05 and watch the curve stand up almost at once. The picture barely changes; the depth range is spent.
